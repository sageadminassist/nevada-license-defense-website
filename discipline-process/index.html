The Nevada Board Discipline Process

What happens after a licensing board complaint — step by step, from Notice of Investigation to appeal.

Call 702.893.4777Free Consultation

Quick answer

Nevada licensing boards handle discipline in roughly eight stages: complaint, Notice of Investigation, investigation, committee review, settlement negotiations, a formal hearing under the Nevada Administrative Procedure Act (NRS Chapter 233B), a decision with any sanctions, and judicial review in district court under NRS 233B.130. Most cases resolve before a hearing. The licensee's first written response matters most — get a lawyer before sending it.

Step by Step

  1. 1

    A complaint is filed

    Most cases start with a complaint from a patient, client, employer, coworker, insurer, or another agency. Boards also open cases on their own after malpractice reports, criminal arrests, failed drug screens, prescription monitoring (PMP) data, or a licensee's own disclosure on a renewal application.

  2. 2

    You receive a Notice of Investigation

    The board notifies you in writing and usually asks for a written response, records, or an interview. Response deadlines are short — often around 14 days. This is the most important moment in the case: what you submit becomes evidence. Do not call the investigator or send a response before speaking with a license defense attorney.

  3. 3

    The board investigates

    An investigator gathers records, interviews witnesses, and may request a mental, physical, or substance-use evaluation. Many boards also use outside expert reviewers to judge whether the standard of care was met.

  4. 4

    A board committee reviews the file

    An investigative or screening committee decides what happens next. Typical options are dismissal, a non-disciplinary letter of warning or concern, or authorizing a formal complaint (also called an accusation or notice of charges).

  5. 5

    Settlement is negotiated — or not

    Most Nevada board cases resolve by agreement before a hearing, through a stipulation or consent agreement. Terms can include education, fines, monitoring, practice restrictions, or probation. A well-prepared defense often means lighter terms or a non-disciplinary outcome.

  6. 6

    Formal hearing under NRS Chapter 233B

    If the case does not settle, it goes to a contested hearing governed by the Nevada Administrative Procedure Act (NRS Chapter 233B). You have the right to an attorney, to present evidence and witnesses, and to cross-examine the board's witnesses. The board, or a hearing officer, then issues written findings and a decision.

  7. 7

    Decision and sanctions

    Possible outcomes range from dismissal to public reprimand, fines and costs, continuing education, probation with monitoring, practice limits, suspension, or revocation. Public discipline is generally posted on the board's website and, for health professionals, reported to the National Practitioner Data Bank (NPDB).

  8. 8

    Judicial review (appeal)

    A licensee who disagrees with a final decision can petition the Nevada district court for judicial review under NRS 233B.130. The petition generally must be filed within 30 days after the final decision is served, so the deadline is easy to miss.

What to Do If You Just Got a Board Letter

  • Don't call the investigator or the person who complained.
  • Don't send a written response yet — note the deadline.
  • Don't change, add to, or destroy any records.
  • Write down your timeline while it's fresh, for your attorney only.
  • Check your malpractice or license-defense insurance — many policies cover board matters.
  • Call a Nevada license defense attorney before the deadline.

Process FAQ

Can a Nevada licensing board suspend my license before a hearing?

Yes, in limited cases. Under NRS 233B.127, a board can order an emergency (summary) suspension when it finds that public health, safety, or welfare requires immediate action. A prompt hearing must follow. Most investigations do not involve a summary suspension.

Is a Notice of Investigation the same as discipline?

No. An investigation is not discipline, and many investigations end in dismissal or a non-disciplinary letter. How you respond at this stage strongly affects whether the case goes further.

Do I have to tell my employer about a board investigation?

It depends on your license, your employment contract, and facility policies. Some obligations are mandatory; others are not. Ask your attorney before disclosing.

Will board discipline show up publicly?

Formal discipline is generally public and appears on the board's license lookup. Dismissals and many non-disciplinary outcomes are not. Health-care discipline is also typically reported to the National Practitioner Data Bank.

How long does the Nevada board discipline process take?

Anywhere from a few months to more than a year, depending on the board, the complexity of the allegations, expert review, and whether the case settles or goes to hearing.
